\lhead{Abstract}
\section*{Abstract}

We forecast the hourly German day-ahead electricity price for the next day using only official data from SMARD, the electricity market data platform of the Bundesnetzagentur. All 27 features are past prices, calendar information or official day-ahead forecasts of load, wind and solar power, so no actual values of the forecast day are used. We compare two simple baselines (a seasonal naive forecast and a linear regression) with a random forest, a neural network and the simple average of the three learned models. Every model is retrained monthly on the previous two years, and the final model was selected on the validation year 2025 before the test data was used.

On the test period January to November 2026, the final model, the equally weighted average of linear regression, random forest and neural network, reaches a mean absolute error (MAE) of 18.18\,EUR/MWh. This is 45.3\,\% lower than the seasonal naive forecast, and the final model wins significantly more test days than every other model (sign test, $p < 0.001$). The best single model changes from month to month, which makes the average the most robust choice. Without the official wind and solar forecasts, which are not guaranteed to be published before the auction, the MAE rises to 29.38\,EUR/MWh: these forecasts carry most of the predictive skill. Feature importance and a partial dependence plot show that the models learn the merit-order relationship between residual load and price. As an extension, a logistic regression detects 92.9\,\% of the negative-price hours of 2026 (PR-AUC 0.84). The main remaining weakness is the underestimation of extreme price spikes.

\textbf{Keywords:} electricity price forecasting, day-ahead market, random forest, forecast averaging, merit order, negative prices

\section{Introduction} \label{Introduction}

In Germany, most electricity for the next day is traded in the day-ahead auction. Every day at 12:00, market participants submit their bids, and the auction determines a price for each delivery period of the following day. These prices are the reference for many contracts and operational decisions. Utilities plan the dispatch of their power plants with them, traders build their positions, battery operators decide when to charge and discharge, and flexible industrial consumers shift their demand to cheaper hours. Good price forecasts therefore have a direct economic value.

Forecasting these prices has become considerably harder in recent years. The share of wind and solar power has grown strongly, the last German nuclear power plants were shut down in April 2023, and the energy crisis of 2021--2022 pushed prices to levels never seen before. In our data, the hourly price ranges from $-500$ to 936\,EUR/MWh, and the number of hours with negative prices grew from 211 in 2019 to 573 in 2025. A forecast that simply repeats yesterday's prices misses most of these dynamics.

\subsection{Problem statement}

We predict the hourly day-ahead price (EUR/MWh) of the bidding zone Germany/Luxembourg for each hour of day $D$. The forecast is made at noon on day $D-1$, so it may only use information that is available at that time (Section~\ref{sec:leakage} discusses one caveat). This is a supervised regression problem on time-series data. Our main evaluation metric is the mean absolute error (MAE).

As an extension, we predict whether an hour will have a negative price. This binary classification problem is relevant for battery operators, flexible consumers and owners of renewable plants, for whom negative prices mean either a revenue opportunity or a cost.

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} How accurately can machine learning models forecast hourly day-ahead prices from official SMARD data alone, compared to a seasonal naive forecast and a linear regression?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How should models be trained when the market changes over time, i.e.\ how much history should be used and which model type is most robust?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Which features drive the forecasts, and do the models learn economically meaningful relationships?
    \item \textbf{RQ4:} How well can negative-price hours be detected in advance?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item A reproducible data pipeline that turns four SMARD files into 27 features without using any actual values of the forecast day.
    \item A comparison of simple and more flexible models from the course (linear regression, random forest, neural network and their average) in a realistic setting with monthly retraining.
    \item A robustness check that quantifies how much the forecasts depend on the official wind and solar forecasts.
    \item An analysis of feature importance and partial dependence that recovers the merit-order curve from data.
    \item A classifier for negative-price hours and a small dashboard to inspect the forecasts.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and the exploratory analysis, and Section~\ref{sec:method} the methodology. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses errors, explanations and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work} \label{sec:related}

\textbf{Electricity price forecasting.} \citet{weron2014} reviews the methods used for electricity price forecasting (EPF) over roughly 15 years, from statistical time-series models to neural networks, and discusses their strengths and weaknesses. He calls for comparative studies that use the same datasets, robust error measures and a careful check of whether one model really outperforms another. We follow these recommendations by using an official, public dataset, the MAE as main metric and a day-by-day comparison of the errors with a simple significance test.

\textbf{Benchmarks and best practices.} \citet{lago2021} note that new EPF methods are often compared on datasets that are not public and on short test periods limited to one market, so it remains unclear which methods work best. They propose guidelines for EPF research, review state-of-the-art algorithms and publish an open-access benchmark with two open-source forecasting models. They recommend a test period of at least one year and comparisons against simple benchmarks. Our test period covers ten and a half months (January to mid-November 2026); together with the validation year 2025, every model is evaluated out-of-sample with monthly retraining on almost two years of data.

\textbf{Model structure and regularization.} \citet{ziel2018} study 12 power markets with 32 regression models estimated via the lasso. They find that shrinkage brings significant accuracy gains compared to commonly used EPF models, and that no single model structure is best across all datasets, seasons and hours. This supports our comparison of several model types and our hour-by-hour error analysis.

\textbf{Forecast combination.} \citet{nowotarski2014} compare schemes for averaging electricity price forecasts. Combining forecasts generally improves accuracy, and equally weighted averaging is a simple but powerful choice when no single model consistently outperforms the others. Our final model uses exactly this equal-weight averaging.

\textbf{Machine learning methods.} Random forests \citep{breiman2001} combine many decision trees, each grown on a random sample of the data with a random selection of features at each split. We implement all models with scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}, the library used throughout the course. To see how a feature affects the forecasts of a complex model, we use partial dependence plots \citep{friedman2001}, which show the average forecast when one feature is changed and all others keep their observed values.

\textbf{Our contribution.} Most of the studies above use data from before 2020. We study the German market from October 2018 to November 2026, a period that includes the energy crisis, the nuclear phase-out and the switch to 15-minute products, and we evaluate how simple models cope with such changes through monthly retraining.

\section{Data} \label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources}

All data comes from SMARD, the electricity market data platform of the Bundesnetzagentur (Federal Network Agency) \citep{smard2026}. It is published under the CC BY 4.0 licence, which permits free use with attribution. We downloaded four files with the settings country Germany, resolution hour, period 01.10.2018 to 17.11.2026 (Table~\ref{tab:files}). Each file has 71,281 hourly rows. Nationwide public holidays were added with the Python package \texttt{holidays}, which contains the official German holiday dates.

The period starts on 1 October 2018, when the joint German-Austrian bidding zone was split and the current German-Luxembourg zone began. The residual load is the load minus wind and solar generation, i.e.\ the demand that conventional power plants have to cover.

\subsection{Data preparation}

All preparation steps are implemented in one module, \texttt{src/data\_pipeline.py}, which every notebook uses. This makes the analysis reproducible from the raw files.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time index.} SMARD reports local time. At the switch to summer time one hour is missing, at the switch to winter time one hour appears twice. Because all files contain exactly one row per hour, we rebuild a clean UTC index and convert it to German time. A check of 26 October 2025 confirms that the repeated hour 02:00 is kept once in summer time and once in winter time.
    \item \textbf{Alignment.} The pipeline checks that all four files have identical timestamps row by row.
    \item \textbf{Missing values.} The consumption forecast is missing for one full day (31 January 2020, 24 hours). We fill it with the forecast of the same hour one week earlier, which has the same weekday profile. All other gaps only affect columns we do not use, e.g.\ nuclear generation after the phase-out or prices of the former German-Austrian zone.
    \item \textbf{15-minute products.} Since 1 October 2025 the day-ahead auction trades 15-minute products. SMARD's hourly values are averages of the quarter-hours, so the hourly series remains consistent over the whole period.
\end{itemize}

After building the lagged features (Section~\ref{sec:method}), the first 30 days are dropped, which leaves 70,538 hourly observations.

\textbf{Autocorrelation.} The autocorrelation of hourly prices has clear peaks at lags of 24 and 168 hours, i.e.\ at the same hour on the previous day and in the previous week. Since the prices of the forecast day itself are unknown at noon on day $D-1$, only lags of at least 24 hours can be used.

\textbf{Implications.} The EDA leads to five modeling decisions: (1) train on recent data and retrain regularly, because the price level shifts; (2) compare a linear model with models that can learn the non-linear merit-order relationship (random forest, neural network); (3) include calendar features for daily, weekly and holiday patterns; (4) include lagged prices of at least 24 hours; and (5) treat negative prices as a separate classification task.

\section{Methodology} \label{sec:method}

\subsection{Leakage rule} \label{sec:leakage}

The auction for day $D$ closes at 12:00 on day $D-1$. A feature for an hour of day $D$ is therefore only allowed if it is known at that time. This rule excludes two variables that would otherwise look very useful: the prices of neighbouring countries, which are determined in the same auction, and actual generation, which is published only afterwards. Allowed are SMARD's day-ahead forecasts for day $D$, all prices up to and including day $D-1$ (which were set in the auction on day $D-2$) and calendar information.

One caveat concerns the publication times of the forecasts. The transmission system operators must publish the day-ahead load forecast at least two hours before the auction closes, but the day-ahead wind and solar forecasts only by 18:00 on day $D-1$ \citep{eu2013transparency}. The wind and solar forecasts, and therefore the residual load, are thus not guaranteed to be available at noon. We use them as a proxy for the weather-based wind and solar forecasts that market participants prepare or buy themselves before the auction. If such forecasts are less accurate than the official ones, our errors are optimistic; Section~\ref{sec:robust} quantifies the worst case without any wind and solar information.

\subsection{Features}

We build 27 features in three groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}). Each is a short pandas operation in \texttt{src/\allowbreak data\_\allowbreak pipeline.py}. All rolling statistics are shifted by 24 hours so that they only use prices up to day $D-1$.

\subsection{Models}

All models come from scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011} and were introduced in the course.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seasonal naive (baselines).} The price at the same hour one day (\texttt{lag24}) or one week (\texttt{lag168}) earlier. Any useful model must beat this.
    \item \textbf{Linear regression (interpretable baseline).} Ordinary least squares on all 27 standardized features.
    \item \textbf{Random forest} \citep{breiman2001}. 200 trees, at least 5 samples per leaf, 50\,\% of the features considered at each split. Trees can learn non-linear effects such as the merit-order curve.
    \item \textbf{Neural network (MLP).} A multi-layer perceptron with two hidden layers (128 and 64 neurons) on standardized features, with early stopping on 10\,\% of the training data.
    \item \textbf{Average of the three models.} The forecast is the plain mean of the linear regression, random forest and MLP forecasts. No weights are estimated, following the finding of \citet{nowotarski2014} that simple averaging is robust.
\end{itemize}

The hyperparameters of the random forest and the MLP were set to common values and not tuned systematically. This keeps the comparison simple; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses it as a limitation.

\subsection{Monthly retraining} \label{sec:rolling}

In practice, a forecasting model is retrained regularly with the newest data. We simulate this with a simple loop: for each month of the evaluation period, every model is retrained on the preceding $N$ days and then predicts all hours of that month. The window length $N$ was chosen on the validation year (Section~\ref{sec:window}). The same procedure is applied to the test year, so the test results reflect how the models would have performed in real use.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MAE} (main metric): the mean absolute error in EUR/MWh. It is easy to interpret and robust to single extreme hours.
    \item \textbf{RMSE}: penalizes large errors more strongly.
    \item \textbf{MAE on extreme hours}: the error on the 5\,\% highest-priced hours (spikes) and on hours with negative prices.
\end{itemize}

We do not use percentage errors such as the MAPE, because prices close to or below zero make them meaningless.

A lower average error can come from a few days on which one model was lucky. We therefore also compute each model's MAE for every single test day and count the days on which the final model is more accurate than each other model. If two models were equally good, each would win about half of the days, like a coin flip. A sign test, i.e.\ a binomial test of the number of days won against 50\,\%, gives the probability of a result at least this extreme under that assumption; we call a difference significant if this p-value is below 0.05.

\subsection{Extension: negative-price classification} \label{sec:classmethod}

The target is 1 if the price is below 0\,EUR/MWh and 0 otherwise. Only about 6\,\% of the hours are negative, so accuracy would be misleading: a model that never predicts a negative price would be 94\,\% accurate. We therefore report the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC, main metric), precision, recall and F1. We compare a simple rule (predict \enquote{negative} if the forecasted renewable share exceeds a threshold chosen on the training data), a logistic regression and a random forest with 300 trees. For each model, the decision threshold that maximizes F1 on the validation year is kept fixed for the test. For the test, all models are retrained on 2024--2025.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why does the model ranking change in 2026?} \label{sec:ranking}

On the validation year the random forest was clearly better than the linear regression (12.97 vs.\ 14.55\,EUR/MWh); on the test year the linear regression was slightly better (20.39 vs.\ 21.00). Figure~\ref{fig:monthly} shows that the ranking is not stable even within 2026: the random forest is the best single model in five months (January, February, April, June and November), the linear regression in five others (March, May, July, August and October) and the MLP in September.

Part of the explanation is the rise of the price level in autumn 2026. The monthly mean price was between 79 and 127\,EUR/MWh from January to August, but climbed to 145\,EUR/MWh in September, 169 in October and 245 in 1--17 November. From September on, the 30-day mean price was higher than at any time in the two-year training window in 43\,\% (September), 98\,\% (October) and 94\,\% (November) of the hours. Tree models cannot extrapolate: each leaf predicts an average of training observations, so their forecasts stay within the range of situations seen before. A linear model can extrapolate along price-level features such as \texttt{lag24} and \texttt{prev\_day\_mean}. Accordingly, the gap between the two is largest in October (26.2 vs.\ 31.0\,EUR/MWh).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_monthly_mae.pdf}
    \caption{MAE by month on the test year 2026; in the shaded months the price level was above the range of the training window}
    \label{fig:monthly}
\end{figure}

The average combines these properties. The random forest captures the non-linear merit order, the linear regression follows shifts in the price level, and the MLP reacts strongly to extreme situations. The average is the best model in 8 of the 11 test months and at most 2.6\,EUR/MWh behind the best single model in the other three. This is exactly the situation in which \citet{nowotarski2014} find equal-weight averaging to be a simple but powerful choice: no individual model consistently outperforms the others. For RQ2 we conclude that monthly retraining on two years of data, combined with a simple average of different models, is the most robust strategy in a changing market.

\subsection{Error analysis}

\textbf{Bias towards the average.} Table~\ref{tab:bins} groups the test errors of the final model by the actual price, and Figure~\ref{fig:errors} shows the error by hour of the day. The model overestimates low and negative prices and underestimates high prices; for prices above 300\,EUR/MWh the mean error is 77\,EUR/MWh. This regression towards the mean is typical when models minimize average errors and extreme hours are rare in the training data. The error is lowest at night (about 13\,EUR/MWh) and largest in the evening hours 19:00--20:00 (about 25\,EUR/MWh), when the price reacts most strongly to the residual load.

\subsection{Which features drive the forecasts?}

Figure~\ref{fig:importance} shows the feature importance of the random forest trained on the latest two years of data. It measures how much each feature reduces the error inside the trees. The forecasted residual load is the most important feature, followed by the renewable share and yesterday's price at the same hour (\texttt{lag24}); together these three account for more than 60\,\% of the total importance. The price-level features (\texttt{same\_hour\_mean\_7d}, \texttt{mean\_30d}, \texttt{prev\_day\_mean}) come next, because they tell the model how expensive the market currently is. Calendar features matter little once the residual load is known, since the residual load already contains the daily and weekly demand pattern. This importance measure favours features with many different values, so it is a rough guide rather than an exact ranking. The coefficients of the linear regression (notebook 02) point in the same direction: \texttt{lag24}, the change of the residual load compared with the previous day and the previous day's mean price have the largest positive effects, the renewable share a negative one.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/fig_feature_importance.pdf}
    \caption{Feature importance of the random forest (15 most important features, trained on November 2024 to November 2026)}
    \label{fig:importance}
\end{figure}

The partial dependence plot \citep{friedman2001} of the residual load (Figure~\ref{fig:pdp}) shows how the forecast changes when only the residual load is varied. The curve has the step shape of the merit order. Below about 20\,GWh the forecast falls steeply, because renewables push conventional plants out of the market and prices can become negative. Between 20 and 40\,GWh the curve is flatter, as lignite and hard coal plants set the price. Above about 45\,GWh it rises faster again, because expensive gas plants are needed. The model has learned this economic relationship from data alone, which answers RQ3 and increases the trust in its forecasts. A linear regression would describe the same relationship with a straight line.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/fig_partial_dependence.pdf}
    \caption{Partial dependence of the random-forest price forecast on the forecasted residual load (2,000 sampled hours of 2026): the learned merit-order curve}
    \label{fig:pdp}
\end{figure}

For the negative-price classifier, the standardized coefficients of the logistic regression show that a low residual load, a smaller residual load than on the previous day, high solar forecasts and low prices on the previous day increase the probability of negative prices. High offshore wind has a negative coefficient once the other features are known. This is a correlation within the data (offshore wind is high mainly in winter nights with high demand and no solar power) and should not be interpreted as a causal effect.

\subsection{Errors of the negative-price classifier}

The classification errors are economically small. False alarms have a median price of 5.2\,EUR/MWh, so the model correctly identifies an oversupply situation in which the price just stays positive. Missed hours have a median price of $-0.24$\,EUR/MWh. Negative hours occur almost exclusively between 9:00 and 17:00 from March to September, which confirms that solar generation is the main driver. The threshold chosen on 2025 favours recall over precision; a battery operator who wants fewer false alarms could choose a higher threshold.

\subsection{Limitations} \label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Missing fuel prices.} Gas and CO$_2$ prices drive the general price level but are not part of the SMARD data. Rolling price features and monthly retraining compensate for this only partly, as the higher errors in 2026 show.
    \item \textbf{Extreme prices.} The models are biased towards the average and underestimate spikes.
    \item \textbf{No systematic tuning.} The random forest and the MLP use common, fixed settings. Tuning them with time-series cross-validation might improve the single models, although the averaging already reduces the dependence on any one model.
    \item \textbf{Resolution.} We forecast hourly averages, while the market has traded 15-minute products since October 2025.
    \item \textbf{Publication time of the renewable forecasts.} The official wind and solar forecasts may be published after the auction closes (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Without any wind and solar information, the MAE of the final model rises to 29.38\,EUR/MWh (Section~\ref{sec:robust}); a forecaster with own weather-based forecasts would lie between this worst case and our main result.
    \item \textbf{Single market and period.} The results apply to Germany/Luxembourg and a test period of less than one year; they may differ for other markets or years.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

We built an end-to-end machine learning system that forecasts hourly German day-ahead electricity prices from official SMARD data, using only day-ahead forecasts, past prices and calendar information, and only models from the course.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (accuracy):} All learned models clearly beat the seasonal naive forecast. The final model, the simple average of linear regression, random forest and neural network, reaches a test MAE of 18.18\,EUR/MWh in 2026, 45.3\,\% below the naive forecast, and wins significantly more test days than every other model. Without any wind and solar information it would reach 29.38\,EUR/MWh, so the renewable forecasts are the most valuable input.
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (changing market):} Monthly retraining on a two-year window works better than one or three years. The best single model changes from month to month; the plain average of different models is the best model in 8 of 11 test months and the most robust choice when the price level shifts.
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (drivers):} The forecasted residual load, the renewable share and yesterday's prices are the most important features. A partial dependence plot shows that the random forest learns the step-shaped merit-order curve from data.
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (negative prices):} A logistic regression detects 92.9\,\% of the negative-price hours in 2026 (PR-AUC 0.84). Most remaining errors occur at prices close to zero, where wrong decisions are cheap.
\end{itemize}

The main weakness is the underestimation of extreme price spikes, partly because fuel and CO$_2$ prices are not part of the data. Promising next steps are hyperparameter tuning with time-series cross-validation, forecasts of price ranges instead of single values, forecasting at the 15-minute resolution of the current market, and using the predicted probabilities of negative prices in a simple battery charging strategy to measure the economic value of the forecasts.

As a prototype, we implemented a Gradio dashboard (\texttt{app/dashboard.py}, Figure~\ref{fig:dashboard}). Users pick any day of the test period and compare the out-of-sample forecasts of all models with the actual prices; three examples (a normal workday, a Sunday with negative prices and the spike of 9 November) are one click away.
